There is a romantic idea in business that being first is inherently valuable.

Find an opportunity before everyone else.

Launch before the competition.

Create a new category.

Own the market.

Sometimes that is exactly what happens.

But there is another side to being first that entrepreneurs do not discuss nearly as often.

Sometimes the first mover does not merely have to build a product.

It has to build the **customer** for the product.

It has to explain what the thing is.

Explain why anybody needs it.

Explain why the old way is no longer sufficient.

Teach people how to use it.

Build trust around an unfamiliar transaction.

Develop distribution that does not yet exist.

Sometimes it even has to help create the infrastructure, regulation, language and cultural acceptance necessary for the product to make sense.

That can become extraordinarily expensive.

The supposed first-mover advantage can quickly become a **first-mover tax**.

Few Nigerian business stories illustrate both sides of this better than Indomie.

## Before Indomie could sell noodles, Nigeria had to learn noodles

When Tolaram began importing Indomie into Nigeria in 1988, it was not entering an established instant-noodle category and attempting to win market share from five competitors.

For practical purposes, it was helping create the category.

Instant noodles were unfamiliar to a large part of the population. Research on Tolaram's Nigerian expansion records something almost difficult to imagine today: some consumers initially thought noodles looked like worms. Tolaram therefore invested heavily in sampling and direct customer education rather than relying solely on conventional advertising. The company adapted flavours to Nigerian tastes and eventually invested deeply in manufacturing, logistics, packaging, distribution and other parts of the value chain necessary to make the category work.

Think about how remarkable that is today.

For millions of Nigerians, particularly younger generations, noodles require no explanation at all.

A parent does not need a presentation on the concept.

A child does not need to understand the history of Asian cuisine.

A retailer does not need to be educated on where to place the product.

The category has become ordinary.

But ordinary is what successful innovation often looks like **after somebody has spent years making it ordinary**.

Tolaram started importing Indomie in 1988 and moved into local manufacturing in the mid-1990s. Over time it built manufacturing capacity, logistics and distribution around the category, while continuing substantial sampling and market-development activity.

What looks in retrospect like a brilliant first-mover advantage was therefore also a massive market-creation exercise.

Tolaram did not simply sell a better noodle.

It helped teach an enormous market to eat noodles.

That distinction matters.

## Market education is an invisible cost

When entrepreneurs calculate the economics of a new product, they naturally think about familiar costs:

Product development.

People.

Technology.

Manufacturing.

Rent.

Distribution.

Marketing.

Capital.

What is much harder to model is the cost of changing behaviour.

Suppose your customer already buys bottled water and you launch another bottled-water brand.

Your problem is difficult, but conceptually straightforward.

The customer understands water.

Understands the bottle.

Understands when to buy it.

Understands roughly what it should cost.

Understands how to consume it.

Your job is primarily to convince the customer to choose **you**.

Now imagine selling something for which the customer must first understand an entirely new concept.

You have two sales to make.

First:

**Why should I do this at all?**

Only then:

**Why should I do it with you?**

That first sale is market education.

And here is the painful part:

If you succeed, the second company entering the market may not have to pay for it.

You teach people what the category means.

You answer the objections.

You establish the vocabulary.

You demonstrate that the model can work.

You normalise the behaviour.

Then a competitor arrives later with a slightly better interface, stronger funding, lower costs or better distribution and sells to the customer you spent years educating.

That is the first-mover tax.

## Tolaram could afford to create a category because it committed to creating the system around it

There is another lesson in the Indomie story that is sometimes overlooked.

Education alone was not enough.

Tolaram had to make the surrounding system work.

The Christensen Institute's examination of the company describes how creating the noodle market required investment beyond the product itself: manufacturing, energy, transportation, distribution and other supporting infrastructure.

This is common in genuinely new markets.

The product may be innovative, but the surrounding ecosystem has not caught up.

So the pioneer must fill the gaps.

Uber cannot function without sufficient drivers.

Digital payments require merchants.

Electric vehicles require charging infrastructure.

Marketplaces require buyers and sellers simultaneously.

A completely new financial product may require customers, regulators, banks and distribution partners to understand it at roughly the same time.

The entrepreneur therefore discovers that the company is not building one business.

It is building several pieces of infrastructure necessary for the main business to exist.

Very large opportunities can justify that investment.

But the entrepreneur should understand what is being undertaken.

## Fractional real estate in Nigeria offers another useful example

The idea is immediately attractive.

Real estate is expensive.

Many Nigerians believe strongly in property as a store of wealth.

Instead of requiring one investor to buy an entire property, divide the economic ownership into smaller portions and allow multiple participants to invest.

On paper, the logic seems almost irresistible.

Nigeria also already has formal structures intended to give investors exposure to real estate without buying an entire building. Real Estate Investment Trusts have existed locally for years, yet uptake has remained relatively limited. More recently, several technology companies have attempted to make fractional participation more accessible through digital platforms.

The challenge has not been that Nigerians need convincing that property is valuable.

The challenge is that **fractional property introduces questions that ordinary property ownership does not immediately answer**.

What exactly do I own?

Whose name is on the title?

Can I visit the property?

Who receives the rent?

Who pays for maintenance?

Who decides when the property is sold?

What happens if another investor wants to exit?

Can I sell my fraction?

Who determines its value?

What happens if the platform disappears?

Am I buying property, shares, units in a scheme, or merely a contractual right to returns?

Who regulates the transaction?

What happens if the developer fails?

Those are not unreasonable questions.

But every additional question increases the amount of education required before the customer reaches a buying decision.

And in financial products, education and trust are inseparable.

## The Nigerian environment makes the education burden even heavier

This is particularly important because fractional investment sits near a regulatory boundary that ordinary property marketing often does not.

Nigeria's Securities and Exchange Commission introduced crowdfunding rules in 2021, including requirements around registered intermediaries, investor protection, due diligence, custody, disclosures and limits. The SEC has also repeatedly warned investors against unregistered online investment schemes.

That is important regulation.

But from the entrepreneur's perspective it means a fractional platform is potentially solving three problems simultaneously:

**Accessibility.**

**Trust.**

**Comprehension.**

The customer needs to understand the property.

Then understand the fractional structure.

Then become comfortable with the institution offering it.

This is a much harder commercial challenge than merely displaying apartments online.

## The experience of the market suggests the problem is real

It would be inaccurate to say fractional real estate has simply failed in Nigeria.

There are active operators, and the category continues to develop.

But the journey has been uneven.

Keble, one of the better-known Nigerian platforms in the category, originally built its proposition substantially around fractional real-estate ownership. Its founders later acknowledged that by 2023 the business had come close to shutting down: the model generated insufficient margins and the company struggled to raise additional capital.

The company subsequently expanded beyond pure fractional investment into co-ownership, outright home ownership, instalment land purchases and mortgage-enabled structures. BusinessDay reported in 2026 that this broader model helped improve its economics and revive growth.

That is not evidence that fractionalisation itself is a bad idea.

In fact, Keble continues to offer fractional ownership today.

It demonstrates something subtler.

Sometimes a technically elegant solution addresses a real problem but still asks the market to move farther from familiar behaviour than the business can economically support.

The answer may not be to abandon the innovation.

It may be to **move the innovation closer to behaviour the customer already understands**.

## This is an important distinction: product innovation versus behavioural innovation

Entrepreneurs naturally want to innovate the product.

But every innovation does not also need to require a new customer behaviour.

There is enormous strategic value in asking:

**What part of this transaction can remain familiar?**

When ride-hailing emerged, the customer still understood the fundamental proposition:

I need a car to take me somewhere.

The innovation changed how the car was found, priced and paid for.

It did not require consumers to first understand an entirely new reason for transportation.

Many of the most successful technologies work this way.

They radically change the machinery underneath an existing behaviour while allowing the customer to continue recognising what they are doing.

That observation is central to how we are approaching **EkoBuja**.

## EkoBuja begins with something Nigerians already understand

EkoBuja is a BlueX business designed to expand participation in quality real estate by enabling fractional access to property opportunities. BlueX describes the platform as co-ownership of income-producing property, in shares that co-owners can trade with each other.

But the more important strategic idea is not simply:

**Let us fractionalise property.**

Others have attempted that.

Our starting question is different:

**How little new behaviour can we ask the customer to learn?**

The Nigerian property market already contains ownership and investment structures that operate at meaningful scale and that customers broadly understand.

People understand buying land.

They understand apartments.

They understand estates.

They understand rental property.

They understand buying units within developments.

They understand paying towards property over time.

They understand the idea that good property can appreciate and generate income.

EkoBuja's opportunity is therefore not to create an exotic new asset class and then spend enormous amounts of capital persuading Nigerians to understand it.

It is to take **property structures and propositions that already make intuitive sense**, then use fractionalisation to reduce the amount of capital required for participation.

The innovation sits in the access layer.

Not necessarily in reinventing the customer's entire understanding of property.

## Fractionalisation should become almost invisible

This is an important product-design principle.

Customers should not have to become experts in fractionalisation before they can become customers.

Imagine two propositions.

The first says:

> Learn about a revolutionary fractional-property investment model through which digital units represent proportional economic exposure to an underlying real-estate asset.

The second says, in effect:

> Here is a property you understand. Here is what it costs. Here is how it earns. You do not have to buy the whole thing to participate.

Both may ultimately describe a similar economic mechanism.

But they impose very different cognitive costs.

Our view is that the second path has significantly greater potential for scale.

The customer begins with something familiar:

**the property.**

The fractional structure simply solves affordability.

## Reduce the education gap before increasing the marketing budget

This is the broader business lesson.

When a product struggles to gain adoption, companies often conclude that they need more advertising.

Sometimes they do.

But sometimes what they really need is **less novelty**.

Every new concept introduced into a buying decision creates what we might call an **education gap**.

The distance between what the customer already understands and what they must understand before they can confidently transact.

Good product strategy should try to reduce that distance.

For EkoBuja, that means building around several principles.

### Start with recognisable assets

The underlying proposition should make sense before the fractional structure is explained.

A good property remains a good property.

Its location, title, use, rental economics, valuation and market should carry the proposition.

Fractionalisation should improve access to the asset, not compensate for a weak asset.

### Use familiar transaction logic

Where possible, customers should encounter concepts they already recognise from ordinary property ownership and investment.

Ownership.

Rent.

Value.

Sale.

Documentation.

Returns.

Costs.

Exit.

The platform's job is to make those things clearer, not bury them underneath fintech vocabulary.

### Let scale happen underneath the customer experience

One of technology's great advantages is that substantial complexity can exist behind an extremely simple interface.

The legal, governance, valuation, payment, reporting and ownership machinery may be sophisticated.

The customer's experience should not have to feel sophisticated.

Complexity is sometimes unavoidable operationally.

It should not automatically be exported to the customer.

### Make trust structural, not promotional

A market with previous failed investment schemes will not be persuaded simply because another startup says it is trustworthy.

Trust must be visible in the structure.

Documentation.

Clear ownership.

Independent verification where appropriate.

Transparent valuation.

Governance.

Defined exit mechanisms.

Proper regulatory treatment.

Clear disclosures.

The customer should not merely be told that the transaction is safe.

The customer should be able to understand **why the structure exists the way it does**.

### Sell the outcome before the mechanism

Consumers rarely buy innovation simply because it is innovative.

They buy outcomes.

Indomie ultimately solved a very simple problem:

A convenient, inexpensive meal that could be prepared quickly.

The customer did not need to admire the supply-chain architecture Tolaram built behind it.

Likewise, a property customer fundamentally wants some combination of:

Ownership.

Wealth preservation.

Rental income.

Capital appreciation.

A future home.

A stake in a valuable location.

The fractional technology is a mechanism for making that outcome more accessible.

It should not become the entire story.

## Sometimes the best innovation arrives second

There is therefore an uncomfortable truth about first movers.

The first company often discovers the customer.

The next company may discover the better way to serve that customer.

This is why being first should never become an objective by itself.

There are situations where the right strategy is to create the category.

Tolaram did it spectacularly.

But Tolaram also demonstrates the resources, patience and execution required.

It spent years educating customers.

It sampled the product at enormous scale.

It localised taste.

It built distribution.

It manufactured locally.

It built pieces of infrastructure around the product.

The reward was extraordinary because the commitment was extraordinary.

Not every business needs to—or should—repeat that journey.

Sometimes a better strategy is to find an existing behaviour, an existing structure or an existing desire and innovate **one layer above it**.

Do not teach customers to want something entirely new if you can give them a dramatically better way to obtain something they already want.

## The question is not simply whether the market needs your innovation

The more useful questions are:

**How much of the market must I personally educate before the product becomes commercially viable?**

**How long will that education take?**

**Who pays for it?**

**Will competitors benefit from the education I finance?**

**Can I anchor the new product to something customers already understand?**

**Can I preserve familiar behaviour while innovating the structure underneath it?**

Those questions matter particularly in emerging markets where capital is expensive and the runway available for prolonged consumer education can be limited.

There is enormous value in innovation.

But there is also value in familiarity.

At BlueX, we think the most interesting opportunities often exist where the two meet.

With EkoBuja, the ambition is not to persuade Nigerians that they should suddenly believe in an unfamiliar form of wealth.

Nigerians already believe in property.

The opportunity is to make participation in the property market more accessible without asking customers to abandon the mental models that made property valuable to them in the first place.

That distinction may ultimately matter more than whether the underlying technology is described as fractional ownership, co-ownership, digital property or anything else.

A market should not have to understand your innovation before it can benefit from it.

Sometimes the smartest way to build the future is to make it feel surprisingly familiar.
